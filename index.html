PART 1

What Is Deviance? 0/5

Deviance — variation from a set of norms or shared social expectations. People who violate these expectations are deviants. The opposite of deviance is conformity.

Deviance isn't automatically bad for a society. The textbook identifies real positive functions of deviance (it defines the limits of social tolerance, increases group solidarity, serves as a safety valve for discontent, points out defects in the social order, and can set social change in motion) alongside real dysfunctions (it disrupts social order, discourages others from conforming, destroys trust, and diverts resources into control efforts).

Traditional Views of Deviance

Absolutist view — certain behaviors are deviant regardless of social context. Moralist view — norm violations are deviant because they are morally wrong. Medical view — deviance is pathological evidence that a society is unhealthy (and medicalization of deviance is the transformation of moral/legal deviance into a medical condition). Statistical view — any variation from a statistical norm counts as deviant (e.g., left-handedness).

PART 3

Deviance and Social Control 0/6

Internal means of control — learned patterns of control that exist in the minds of individuals and make them want to conform. External means of control — pressures or sanctions applied to members of society by others. Sanctions — rewards and punishments used to encourage proper behavior (positive sanctions encourage a behavior to continue; negative sanctions discourage it).
Informal external controls — positive and negative controls, like smiling, frowning, and high-fiving, used to influence behavior. Formal external controls — the systems created by society specifically to control deviance, such as those in a courtroom.

PART 4

Theories Explaining Deviance: Biology & Psychology 0/4

Before sociologists developed their own explanations, biological and psychological theories dominated. Most have been seriously undermined by later research — but they still shape popular thinking about crime today.

Cesare Lombroso, the "father of modern criminology," argued criminals were a "born criminal" type identifiable by physical traits (long arms, small ears, a protruding jaw). William Sheldon later linked body type to behavior: endomorphs (soft, round) as laid-back, ectomorphs (skinny) as shy and cerebral, and mesomorphs (muscular) as disproportionately criminal. Research has basically ruled out a "born criminal," and most sociologists reject biology or heredity alone as a cause of deviance.
Sigmund Freud's psychoanalytic theory divides personality into the id (the inborn, pleasure-seeking drive), the superego (internalized social norms — our "conscience"), and the ego (the "reality principle" that mediates between the two). An id-dominated personality is more likely to be deviant; a superego-dominated one is likely to be rigid.

PART 5

Sociological Theories of Deviance 0/9

Structural Functionalism: Strain & Anomie

Anomie — the incongruence between a society's emphasis on attaining certain goals and the availability of legitimate, institutionalized means of reaching those goals; often gives rise to deviance. Robert Merton identified five modes of adaptation to the gap between culturally approved goals (wealth, success) and the legitimate means of achieving them (education, hard work): Conformity (accepts both goals and means), Innovation (accepts goals, rejects legitimate means), Ritualism (rejects goals, rigidly follows means), Retreatism (rejects both), and Rebellion (rejects and replaces both with new ones).

Match each person to their mode of adaptation.

Conflict Theory

Conflict theory — a social theory that views conflict as inevitable and natural and as a significant cause of social change. Conflict theorists argue the powerful define the acts of weaker groups as "deviant," and that the law tends to favor the rich and powerful — not because officials are evil, but because punishing the middle and upper classes would cost them political and economic support.

Symbolic Interactionism: Learning & Labeling

Cultural transmission theory (subculture theory) — a community's deviance may be transmitted to newcomers through learning and socialization. Differential association theory (Sutherland) — deviance results when individuals have more contact with groups that define deviance favorably than with groups that define it unfavorably. Social learning theory — deviant and conforming behaviors are strongly influenced by their consequences (rewards and punishments) — differential reinforcement.
Labeling theory — emphasizes how certain behaviors are labeled "deviant," and how being given that label influences a person's future behavior. Edwin Lemert distinguished primary deviance (temporary, sporadic norm violation the person doesn't see as part of their identity) from secondary deviance (habitual violation where the person is labeled — by themselves and others — as deviant, which often becomes a self-fulfilling "deviant career").

PART 6

Crime & the Criminal Justice System 0/6

Crime — a violation of criminal statutory law, typically accompanied by a specific punishment applied by a governmental authority. Crimes are classified as felonies (punishable by 1+ years in state prison) or misdemeanors (a fine and/or up to 1 year in county jail).

Match each example to its type of crime: violent, property, victimless, white-collar, or organized crime.

Measuring Crime

Uniform Crime Report (UCR) — the FBI's official source for crime statistics, gathered from 18,000+ law enforcement agencies, but limited to crimes actually reported to police. National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS) — data collected by the Bureau of Justice Statistics directly from victims (even unreported crimes) — and in 2023, only about 45% of violent crimes were reported to police at all.

A researcher wants to know how much crime actually happens, including crimes victims never reported to police. Which source should they rely on?

The Criminal Justice System

The criminal justice system has three parts: police (local, county, state, and federal), courts (a dual system of state and federal courts, up through appellate courts and, ultimately, the U.S. Supreme Court), and corrections (incarceration in jails, state prisons, federal prisons, and private prisons, plus community corrections like probation, parole, home confinement, and restitution). With over 1.9 million people in prison and over 6 million under some form of correctional supervision, the United States incarcerates more people than any other country in the world.

Behind the Bars: Power, Conformity, and Deviance

A Sociological Reflection

In 1971, psychologist Philip Zimbardo randomly assigned college-student volunteers to play "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock prison in the basement of a Stanford University building. The study was meant to run two weeks; Zimbardo shut it down after six days, after guards escalated to humiliating and abusive treatment of prisoners and several prisoners suffered severe emotional distress.

Video provided by Professor Scott for this assignment

A note on the study's criticisms: The Stanford Prison Experiment is one of the most famous studies in social science — and also one of the most heavily criticized. Researchers who later reviewed Zimbardo's original audio recordings and archived materials found evidence that guards were coached toward cruelty rather than simply discovering it on their own: a staff member instructed guards, before the study began, to be "tough" and to create a sense of powerlessness in the prisoners. Critics have also pointed out that participants were not a random cross-section of the public (they responded to an ad for a study on "prison life," which may have selected for certain personality traits), and that several guards later said they were partly performing for the researchers. None of this means the study revealed nothing — but it does mean its most dramatic conclusion, that ordinary people will spontaneously and uniformly become cruel the moment they're given power over others, is more contested than the popular retelling suggests.

Key Takeaways — Chapter 7

  • Deviance is variation from shared norms. It has both positive functions (defining tolerance, building solidarity, acting as a safety valve, exposing social defects, driving change) and dysfunctions (disrupting order, destroying trust).
  • Traditional views treat deviance as absolute/moral, medical/pathological, or statistical. The relativistic view, which most sociologists hold, says deviance can only be understood in the social context where it happens — it varies by time, place, situation, and social status.
  • Social control relies on internal controls (conscience, self-image) and external controls, both informal (peer pressure, a frown) and formal (police, courts).
  • Biological (Lombroso, Sheldon, XYY) and psychological (Freud's id/ego/superego) theories have mostly been discredited as sole explanations, though some sociologists accept that biology can combine with social factors.
  • Structural functionalism (Merton's strain/anomie and five modes of adaptation), conflict theory (the powerful define deviance to control the weak), and symbolic interactionism (cultural transmission, differential association, social learning, and labeling theory) are the three major sociological perspectives on deviance.
  • Crime is classified as violent, property, victimless, white-collar, or organized, and measured through the UCR (reported crime) and the NCVS (victimization, including unreported crime). The criminal justice system is made up of police, courts, and corrections.
  • The Stanford Prison Experiment remains one of the most cited — and most contested — studies of power and conformity in institutions, raising real questions about labeling, authority, and accountability that go well beyond the original study.
